Verification messages can confirm that an inbox receives mail, but receiving a link does not prove that the service sending it is trustworthy. Treat sign-in and verification messages as requests to inspect, not instructions to follow automatically.

## Check the context first

If you started the sign-up, a confirmation message may be expected. If you did not, avoid clicking links or opening unexpected attachments. Visit the service by typing its known address into your browser rather than relying on an unsolicited link.

Read the sender and destination carefully. Display names can be misleading, and a link's visible text may not match the site it opens. When in doubt, do not use the message.

## Keep important accounts recoverable

An account tied to an address that expires can become difficult—or impossible—to recover. Use a mailbox you control for accounts involving money, personal records, work, health, or long-term access. Keep recovery options current and use a unique password for every account.

A temporary mailbox can be useful for testing a non-production email flow. It is a poor place to store recovery codes or important correspondence.

## Understand inbox exposure

Disposable mailbox services may have different access and retention models from a personal provider. Do not assume that a message is private just because it is visible in one browser tab. Consider the address, the message, and the provider together before using it.

## If a message seems suspicious

Do not provide passwords or verification codes in response to an email. Use the organization's official website or support channel to check whether the request is genuine.
